{# Amps and the contacts, and nothing derived from voltage. The Shelly's own supply is a 120 V outlet, not a tap off the phase the clamps are around, so its voltage reading belongs to a different circuit. Current does not care: a CT measures the field around a conductor and is independent of what the meter is using as a voltage reference. Watts and power factor very much do care, because real power needs voltage and current from the same phase with the right angle between them, and there is no reason to think that angle is right here. So they are not shown. Typical load is the median of every reading above the running threshold over the last week, which ignores the starting surge without needing a rule about what counts as one. Beside it, how that compares with the four weeks before, because one number is not information: sixteen amps is fine or alarming entirely depending on what it was last month. Pump {{ number }}

Load now is what the clamp reads this second, usually zero on a pit that runs for seconds at a time.

Last run and runs today come from the load rising off nothing. Read the count as a floor: the meter reports on its own schedule, so two close runs can arrive as one. The panel's run contact makes it exact. After three days an ordinary day appears beside it.

Typical load is the middle reading while the pump was running this week, less the starting surge. It is the number to watch: drawing more than it did a month ago is a pump on its way to a problem. Beside it is the change against the four weeks before.

{# The contacts: what is lit now, and under each one what it has been doing. A lamp on its own cannot say the second part. One that is off looks exactly the same whether it went twenty times today or has never gone at all, and that was the whole reason for the three cards this replaces. Different windows on purpose, and each says which it is counting. A float closes every time the pit fills, so a day is the useful number. An alarm that went twice in a month is the whole story, and counting those by the day would read zero forever and teach somebody to stop looking. Overloads have their own heading rather than sitting under Alerts. They are a different kind of bad news: an alert means read the panel, an overload means a pump is off and staying off. The run contacts have no lamp. A pump that is running says so in the column above, in amps, and a lamp repeating that is one more thing to read for nothing. Which input drives which lamp is a setting. There is nothing below this. The panel brings out eight contacts and the module has eight inputs, so every one of them carries a lamp or a run signal and there is no such thing as a leftover to list. #}

Alerts

Alert is the controller's own alarm. Several faults raise the same contact, so it means read the panel rather than anything more specific.

High water means the pit came up past the high float, usually a storm putting water in faster than one pump takes it out. Once in a while is the system working. Regularly is the pit losing to the inflow.

Counted by the month. An alarm counted by the day reads zero forever.

Overloads

A motor overload has tripped: that pump is off and will not run. The panel cut it for drawing more than the overload allows.

The selector decides what happens next. H is hand reset: it stays off until somebody presses the red button back in. A resets itself once cool, which is often worse, a motor that trips and restarts all night being one nobody hears about. O adds a stop, so HO and AO are those two with a stop button.

It tripped for a reason, and a pump drawing more than it used to will show that in its typical load.
