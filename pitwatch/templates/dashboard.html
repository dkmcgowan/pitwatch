{# Amps and the contacts, and nothing derived from voltage. The Shelly's own supply is a 120 V outlet, not a tap off the phase the clamps are around, so its voltage reading belongs to a different circuit. Current does not care: a CT measures the field around a conductor and is independent of what the meter is using as a voltage reference. Watts and power factor very much do care, because real power needs voltage and current from the same phase with the right angle between them, and there is no reason to think that angle is right here. So they are not shown. Typical load is the median of every reading above the running threshold over the last week, which ignores the starting surge without needing a rule about what counts as one. Beside it, how that compares with the four weeks before, because one number is not information: sixteen amps is fine or alarming entirely depending on what it was last month. #} {# Two rows rather than four. Pump {{ number }}

The word beside the name is the controller's own, and it means what it means on the panel door. LEAD is the pump that answers the next call, and the one running while it runs. LAG is the one sitting out, which is whichever pump went last. ON on both means the pit came up past the lag float and the controller called them both. FAIL is an overload: that pump is off and staying off.

Load is what the clamp reads this second, usually zero on a pit that runs for seconds at a time. Beside it is the typical load, which is the middle reading while the pump was running this week, less the starting surge. That is the number to watch: drawing more than it did a month ago is a pump on its way to a problem. When it moves, the change against the four weeks before appears after it.

Runs is when it last started, how many times it has started in the last day, and how many times it starts on an ordinary day. All three come from the load rising off nothing. Read the count as a floor: the meter reports on its own schedule, so two close runs can arrive as one. The panel's run contact makes it exact. The ordinary day is the last month's starts over the days actually recorded, and it appears once there are three of them.

Alerts

System alert is the controller's own alarm. Several faults raise the same contact, so it means read the panel rather than anything more specific.

High water means the pit came up past the high float, usually a storm putting water in faster than one pump takes it out. Once in a while is the system working. Regularly is the pit losing to the inflow.

An overload is the worst of the four, because it is the only one that has already taken a pump away: the panel cut that motor for drawing more than the overload allows, and it is off and will not run.

The selector decides what happens next. H is hand reset: it stays off until somebody presses the red button back in. A resets itself once cool, which is often worse, a motor that trips and restarts all night being one nobody hears about. O adds a stop, so HO and AO are those two with a stop button.

It tripped for a reason, and a pump drawing more than it used to will show that in its typical load.

Counted by the month. An alarm counted by the day reads zero forever.

The words beside the heading are the four rows added up. All clear means every input assigned here is being read and none of them is raised. It counts what it can see, so an alert with no input assigned is not in it, and the lamp for that row is drawn dim to say so.
