{# Amps and the contacts, and nothing derived from voltage. The Shelly's own supply is a 120 V outlet, not a tap off the phase the clamps are around, so its voltage reading belongs to a different circuit. Current does not care: a CT measures the field around a conductor and is independent of what the meter is using as a voltage reference. Watts and power factor very much do care, because real power needs voltage and current from the same phase with the right angle between them, and there is no reason to think that angle is right here. So they are not shown. Two sections, and the split is what each answers. The panel above is the panel: what the controller and the floats are doing. These cards are the pumps themselves, from the clamps, which is both what one is drawing now and how it has been running. Typical load is the median of every reading above the running threshold over the last week, which ignores the starting surge without needing a rule about what counts as one. Under it, how that compares with the four weeks before, because one number is not information: sixteen amps is fine or alarming entirely depending on what it was last month. The run contact and the overload used to sit here too, and the disagreement between the contact and the clamp was the useful part: a closed contactor drawing nothing is a motor that is not turning. Putting them back needs a way to say which input carries pump 1's run contact, and inputs are free text labels now. See NOTES.md. #}

Pump {{ number }}

Current is what the clamp is reading right now, which on a pit that runs for seconds at a time is usually zero.

Last run and runs today come from the current rising off nothing. Once there are three days of history, an ordinary day appears beside today's count, so today has something to be read against.

Read the count as a floor rather than a tally. The meter reports on its own schedule, so two runs close together can arrive looking like one, and the count is at least this many rather than exactly this many. Wiring the panel's run contact makes it exact, because the panel module reports the moment the contact moves.

Typical load is the middle reading of every one taken while the pump was running this week, leaving out the first reading of each run, which is where the starting surge lands. It is the number to watch: a pump drawing more than it did a month ago is a pump on its way to a problem.

FLA is the full load amps printed on the motor plate. Nothing is calculated from it. It is here so the typical load has something real to sit against. Motor overloads are usually set somewhere between 115 and 125 percent of FLA, so a typical load above that is a pump running closer to its trip point than it should be, and worth explaining rather than watching.

Alerts

Alert is the controller's own alarm. It carries no detail: several different faults raise the same contact, so it means go and read the panel rather than anything more specific.

High water means the pit came up past the high water float, which usually happens when a storm is putting water in faster than one pump takes it out. The controller can call both pumps to keep up. Once in a while is the system working. Regularly is the pit struggling with the inflow, and that is worth understanding before it happens on a night nobody is watching.

Counted by the month, because counting an alarm by the day reads zero forever.

Overloads

A motor overload has tripped, so that pump is off and will not run. The panel cut it because it was drawing more than the overload is set to allow.

What happens next is set by the selector on the overload itself. H is hand reset: the pump stays off until somebody goes to the panel and presses the red button back in. A is auto reset: it resets itself once it has cooled and the pump can start again on its own, which sounds better and is often worse, because a motor that trips and restarts all night is a motor nobody hears about. Adding the O makes the red button work as a stop button as well, so HO is hand reset with a stop and AO is auto reset with a stop.

Either way it tripped for a reason. A pump that has been drawing more than it used to will show that in its typical load.
