Panel button

These close a contact across the button on the panel door, so pressing one here is the same as pressing that one. The panel reads how long it is held and nothing else.

Silence stops the horn. The alarm is still up and the pump is still out; nothing has been dealt with.

Reset clears the alarm, which is what puts a pump that has tripped back into the rotation. Until somebody does it the building runs on one pump, and nobody inside can tell.

Neither resets an overload. That is a separate button inside the enclosure, and it is separate on purpose: something is wrong with a motor that has overloaded. Read the amps on this page before you press anything.

{# Amps and the contacts, and nothing derived from voltage. A meter's own supply is often an ordinary outlet rather than a tap off the phase the clamps are around, so its voltage reading belongs to a different circuit. Current does not care: a CT measures the field around a conductor and is independent of what the meter is using as a voltage reference. Watts and power factor very much do care, because real power needs voltage and current from the same phase with the right angle between them, and there is no reason to think that angle is right here. So they are not shown. Typical load is the median of every reading above the running threshold over the last week, which ignores the starting surge without needing a rule about what counts as one. Beside it, how that compares with the four weeks before, because one number is not information: sixteen amps is fine or alarming entirely depending on what it was last month. #} {# Two rows rather than four. The word beside the name is the controller's own. Lead answers the next call and runs. Lag sits this one out. On for both means the pit came up past the lag float and the controller called them both. Fail is an overload: that pump is off and staying off.

Load is what the clamp reads this second, usually zero on a pit that runs for seconds. Beside it is the typical load while running this week, less the starting surge. Drawing more than it did a month ago is the number to watch.

Runs is when it last started, for how long, and how many times today, measured from the panel's own run contact, so the count is a tally rather than an estimate. The ordinary day appears once there are three days to average.

With no run contact wired it falls back to the load rising off nothing, and then the count is a floor rather than a tally: two close runs can arrive as one, and no duration is offered at all.

Alerts

System alert is the controller's own alarm. Several faults raise the same contact, so it means read the panel.

High water means the pit came up past the high float. Once in a while is the system working. Regularly is the pit losing to the inflow.

An overload has already taken a pump away: the panel cut that motor and it will not run. The selector says what happens next. H stays off until somebody presses the red button back in. A resets itself once cool, which is often worse. O adds a stop, so HO and AO are those two with a stop button.

Both pumps is the two run signals closed at once, which is the controller calling the lag pump because one could not keep up.

Counted by the month, because an alarm counted by the day reads zero forever. All clear counts only the inputs that are assigned and being read; a row with no input is drawn dim.

Rain

Hour by hour over the pit, a day behind and a day ahead, with the line marking now. Left of it is what fell, right of it is what is expected.

This is why an ejector pit gets busy. Groundwater keeps rising for a day or two after the rain stops, so a night of calls after a wet afternoon is the pit doing its job, and the same night under a dry chart is a question.

Model numbers rather than a gauge on the roof, from Open-Meteo, right enough to answer "was it raining". Snow counts as the water it holds, which reaches the pit when it melts.

"Not set up" means there is nowhere to look yet: set the location in settings and turn the rain on.

Tide

Water level at the nearest NOAA gauge, most of a day either side of now, with the line marking now. The solid trace is what the gauge measured, the faint one is the harmonic prediction.

A pit below the water table is a hole with the water table in it. On tidal ground that table moves with the tide, so a pit near the water fills faster at high water, and a rise in calls that tracks the tide is groundwater rather than anything in the building.

The surge is how far the water is above or below prediction. A foot the moon did not put there is wind or weather pushing water inshore, and it is what floods a cellar.
