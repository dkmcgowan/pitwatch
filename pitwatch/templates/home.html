PitWatch

Monitoring and alerting for a duplex ejector pump panel. It watches the pumps, and when something is wrong it tells the people responsible for them.

An ejector pit sits below the sewer line in a basement, and a pump lifts what collects there up to it. When those pumps fail, the failure is quiet. Nothing alarms, nothing is displayed, and the first anybody knows about it is water coming up through a floor. PitWatch exists to make that failure loud, early, and reach a phone.

What it watches

A current clamp on each motor, and the panel's own contacts: the floats, the run signals, the motor overloads and the panel alarm. From those it knows what each pump is drawing, how long it ran, how often it runs, and whether the panel itself is complaining.

That combination is the point. Current alone says a pump is running but not why it started. Contacts alone say a float is wet but not that the motor is drawing half again what it did last month. Together they catch a pump on its way out rather than one that has already gone.

What it sends

Text messages and emails, to a short list of people entered by the administrator of the building it is installed in. Every message is an operational alert about the equipment: an overload tripped, the high water float is wet, a pump has run far longer than a normal cycle, the monitoring hardware has stopped reporting. A matching message goes out when the condition clears.

There is no marketing of any kind, and no public sign up. A phone number reaches this system only by an administrator entering it on behalf of somebody who agreed to it. Replying STOP to any message ends them. In normal operation the equipment is quiet and nothing is sent at all, sometimes for months.

The Terms & Conditions set all of that out in full, including consent, frequency and how to stop.

PitWatch itself is open source, published under the MIT license at github.com/dkmcgowan/pitwatch. Anybody can run their own copy on their own hardware. Each installation holds its own data and sends its own messages; there is no central service and nothing is shared between them.

Where the data goes

Nowhere. Readings, alert history and the recipient list live on the machine the building runs this on. Nothing is sold, rented or shared, and the only thing that leaves is the alert itself, handed to a mail server or a messaging provider so it can be delivered. See the privacy notice.
