Tide

A pit below the water table is a hole with the water table in it, and near tidal water the water table moves with the tide. If the pit is within a mile or so of a tidal river, bay or harbor, this is worth having: a rise in calls that follows high water is groundwater rather than anything in the building.

The numeric id of a water level gauge. Press Find nearest, or look one up at tidesandcurrents.noaa.gov.

Only so the pages can name it. Nothing is looked up with this.

Stored in feet above MLLW either way, which is the datum every tide table is published against.

{# The water table itself, which is what the tide above is a proxy for. Its own section rather than a field on the tide's, because it is a different source answering a different question on a different clock: one reading a day, published about a month late. Worth having even where the tide is not, because a pit nowhere near tidal water still sits in groundwater. #}

Groundwater

The USGS runs monitoring wells all over the country and publishes what they read, free and without an account. Where one is close enough, it measures the thing the tide only stands in for: the height of the water table the pit sits in. Useful even a long way from the coast.

It runs about a month behind. These are daily readings published late, so nothing here is live and the dashboard card prints the date each reading belongs to. That suits the question it answers, which is whether this season is unusual rather than what the water is doing this morning.

The site number of a groundwater well, which is fifteen digits rather than the eight a river gauge carries. Press Find nearest, or look one up at waterdata.usgs.gov.

Only so the pages can name it. Nothing is looked up with this.

{# Pressing the panel's own button from somewhere else. One signal, two meanings. The reference panel reads how long 24 V is held on the controller's reset input and nothing else: a tap silences, three seconds clears. Measured at the panel on 2026-09-12 with a meter, which is why the two durations are fields rather than constants: somebody else's panel will want different ones. Off until somebody has wired a contact across that button and watched it move. #}

Panel button

A pump that has tripped does not rejoin the rotation until the panel alarm is cleared by hand, so the building runs on one pump until somebody walks to the basement. This closes a contact across the button on the panel door, so that walk can happen from a phone instead.

It does not reset the overload. That is a separate button inside the enclosure, and it is separate on purpose: something is wrong with a motor that has overloaded, and it should be looked at before it runs again. Read the amps first.

A Shelly takes JSON-RPC on its device id with /rpc on the end. Turn on RPC over MQTT on the device or it will publish its readings and ignore anything sent to it.

Which relay on it. Nearly always the only one.

A tap. Short enough that the panel does not read it as the long press.

The long press that clears the alarm. Three seconds on the reference panel, so this is a little over.

{# Pressing both without being asked. Off by default and limited when on. In hand reset mode the loop cannot run: the relay stays tripped until a person presses it. In auto reset mode it can, and a loop around a motor that overloads because something is wrong with it is how one pump out of service becomes two burned out. #}

Stops the horn whenever the panel raises an alarm, of any kind. Nothing is hidden: the alarm stays up, the alert stays open and the message still goes out. All it stops is a beacon sounding in a basement at nobody.

Clearing the alarm is what puts a tripped pump back into the rotation. Until it happens the building runs on one pump.

Trips before it stops trying, per pump. Past this the alarm is left up and the message says how many times. Zero means no limit: keep clearing it however often it happens.

A pump that trips once has had a bad afternoon. Four times in an hour has something wrong with it.

AI Health Summary

Whether each device is online. The pump and contact names, and the prompt below.

Nothing else: no address, no site name, no coordinates, no account names, and no individual readings. Where it goes is the API address below.

What the pit does, what the pumps are, how the panel is set up, what has been repaired and when. Everything the readings cannot say.

Write one on a schedule

A week read every 7 days is the useful one and is what these start on; 30 days read every 30 days is a trend. The time is on the building's clock, and one whose hour passed while this was down is written when it comes back rather than skipped.

Emailed rather than texted: this is four paragraphs, and four paragraphs is several text messages on the channel that exists here for two in the morning. Only the most recent summary is kept, so a scheduled one replaces the one before it.
